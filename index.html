Characters — tap a card to flip it

The front tells you who they are. The back tells you what to actually write in a test: key actions, what they reveal, and how they connect to Christopher.

Golden rule for character questions: never just name a trait. Trait + evidence from the extract + effect. "Christopher is logical" earns little. CHAPTER 7

How the book is written

Style and structure questions are where students lose easy marks. IEB loves asking about the effect of the author's choices — not just spotting them.

First-person narrator very likely

We experience everything through Christopher's mind. He reports exactly what he sees and never interprets other people's feelings for us — so the reader often understands things (like Ed's guilt, or Judy's letters) before Christopher does. That gap between what he tells us and what we work out is called dramatic irony, and it is the engine of the whole novel.

Prime-number chapters easy 2 marks

Chapters run 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13… Christopher finds prime numbers logical and comforting, so the structure itself mirrors his mind: the book is organised the way he organises the world.

Diagrams, maps & maths effect question

The drawings, timetables, maps and maths problems are not decoration. They show how Christopher processes the world — visually, logically, in systems. When the plot gets emotionally overwhelming, he often retreats into maths; the maths sections are a coping mechanism on the page.

Detective-story frame discuss

Christopher sets out to write a murder mystery about a dog. The twist: the real mystery turns out to be his own family. The detective genre gets flipped — the "detective" is the person the biggest secret was kept from.

Plain, literal language comment on

Christopher avoids metaphors because he finds them illogical (he says they're basically lies), though he accepts similes because they compare truthfully. His flat, precise sentences make the emotional moments hit harder — the language stays calm while the events are devastating.

Red cars & yellow cars symbol

Seeing 4 red cars in a row makes it a Good Day; 4 yellow cars makes it a Black Day. It looks random, but Christopher points out that other people's moods are shaped by arbitrary things too (like the weather). It's his way of imposing order on an unpredictable world — a mini version of the novel's biggest theme.

Formula for any "comment on the effect" question: name the technique → say what it shows about Christopher → say what it makes the reader feel or realise. Three steps, three marks.
CHAPTER 11

Question decoder

The command word tells you how much work the question wants. Match your answer length to the marks: roughly one made point per mark.

Identify / State / Name 1 mark

Short factual answer. No discussion needed. Don't waste time writing paragraphs here.

Explain 2 marks

Give the reason AND develop it. "Because Ed lied" is half an answer; add what the lie was and why it matters.

Discuss 3 marks

A developed answer with evidence from the text. Usually needs 2–3 linked points.

Comment on 2–3 marks

Explain the significance or effect — of a word, an image, a technique. Never just describe; say what it achieves.

Compare 3–4 marks

Similarities AND/OR differences, dealt with side by side — not two separate mini-essays.

Critically discuss / Evaluate 4 marks

Form a judgement and defend it with evidence. IEB rewards answers that see both sides before concluding.

Do you agree? Justify. 3–4 marks

You may agree, disagree, or partly agree — the marks are for the proof, not the position. "Partly agree" answers often score best because they show nuance.

In your own words trap!

If you copy the extract's wording, you get zero — even if the content is right. Rephrase completely.

IEB habit to build now: quote briefly and weave it into your sentence, don't dump long chunks. Short, well-chosen references beat long copied ones every time. And always answer in full sentences unless the question says otherwise.

CHAPTER 17

Mock cycle test

In the real test you'll get a printed extract. Here, each scene is described — picture the page in front of you. Write your answer in the box before revealing the memo. Total: 35 marks. Give yourself 45 minutes.

Section A — The discovery (10 marks)

Scene for questions 1.1–1.4
Imagine the extract is the opening scene: it is after midnight, and Christopher finds Wellington lying on the grass in Mrs Shears' garden with a garden fork through him. He touches the dog, holds him, and decides to find out who killed him. Mrs Shears comes out screaming; the police arrive; when a policeman grabs Christopher's arm, he hits him.
1.1 Place this extract in context: what is the situation at this point in the novel? (2)
Memo: This is the very opening of the novel (✓). Christopher has left his house at night and discovered his neighbour's dog dead in her garden — the event that launches his murder investigation and, eventually, the uncovering of his own family's secrets (✓).
1.2 Explain why Christopher hits the policeman. (2)
Memo: Christopher cannot bear being touched, especially by strangers (✓). Being grabbed overloads and panics him, so hitting out is a defensive, instinctive reaction — not aggression or disrespect (✓).
1.3 Discuss what this extract reveals about Christopher's character. (3)
Memo — any three, developed:
  • He is logical and methodical: he treats the death as a problem to be investigated, not just an upsetting event (✓).
  • He is compassionate in his own way: he holds the dog and cares about what happened to it (✓).
  • He is literal and honest: he answers the police exactly and truthfully (✓).
  • He struggles with sensory overload and touch, which explains his reaction to the policeman (✓).
1.4 Comment on the effectiveness of opening the novel with this scene. (3)
Memo: It hooks the reader with a mystery immediately (✓); it introduces Christopher's unusual, precise way of seeing the world before we're even told anything about him (✓); and it plants the question — who killed Wellington? — whose answer will detonate the family plot later (✓).

Section B — The letters (12 marks)

Scene for questions 2.1–2.4
Imagine the extract is the scene where Christopher, searching for his confiscated book, finds a hidden collection of letters in his father's cupboard — letters addressed to him, in his mother's handwriting, dated after the time he was told she died. He reads them, understands that his mother is alive in London, and becomes physically ill from the shock. Ed finds him, and later confesses: he lied about Judy's death, and he killed Wellington.
2.1 State TWO things Christopher learns from finding the letters. (2)
Memo: His mother is alive and living in London with Mr Shears (✓); his father has been lying to him about her death for two years (✓).
2.2 Explain why Ed's confession about Wellington terrifies Christopher more than it reassures him. (3)
Memo: Christopher reasons with strict logic: his father killed Wellington (✓); therefore his father is capable of killing (✓); therefore, since his father also lied to him, Christopher can no longer trust him and concludes he himself might be in danger — which is why he decides he cannot stay in the house (✓).
2.3 "Ed lied to protect Christopher." Do you agree? Justify your answer. (4)
Memo — a "partly agree" answer scores best:
  • Yes, partly: Ed was overwhelmed, hurt by Judy leaving, and believed the lie would spare Christopher pain he couldn't process (✓✓).
  • But the lie was also self-protective — it hid Ed's own failures and anger — and it violates the thing Christopher values most: truth (✓).
  • Conclusion: the motive was mixed; love and cowardice at the same time, which is exactly why the novel refuses simple "good/bad" labels (✓).
2.4 Discuss how this scene develops the theme of truth and lies. (3)
Memo: Christopher cannot lie and depends on the world being truthful (✓); the letters prove that the person he relied on most built his entire reality on a lie (✓); so the theme moves from an abstract preference into the force that destroys trust and drives the second half of the plot — his journey to London (✓).

Section C — Mini essay (13 marks)

3. "By the end of the novel, Christopher has proven that he can do anything." Critically discuss this statement with reference to his journey to London and the ending of the novel. (13)
Marking guide — what a top answer includes:
  • A clear position, ideally nuanced: he hasn't proven he can do anything, but he has shattered his own (and others') beliefs about his limits.
  • The journey as evidence: London is everything Christopher fears — noise, crowds, strangers, chaos — yet he navigates the station, the train and the Underground alone, using logic and courage to survive an environment designed to overwhelm him.
  • The A-level Maths result as proof of ability in the world's terms, and the puppy, Sandy, as a sign of Ed slowly rebuilding trust.
  • Balance: he still needs routine and support; the trust with his father is repaired step by tiny step, not restored. Growth, not transformation.
  • The final lines' significance: Christopher lists what he has done — solved the mystery, travelled to London alone, written a book — and concludes it means he can do anything. Whether the reader fully agrees or not, his new self-belief is real, and that change is the point of the novel.
  • Structure marks: intro with a stance, PEE paragraphs (Point–Evidence–Explanation), conclusion that answers the question. No storytelling without argument.
After marking yourself: anywhere you lost marks, don't just read the memo — rewrite your answer properly. That rewrite is where the actual learning happens.
